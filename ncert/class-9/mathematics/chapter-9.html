Chapter 9 · Ganita Manjari Part II

Propositions and their Converses

Complete solutions for all Think and Reflect questions and Exercise Set 9.1.

Think and Reflect

Think and Reflect

Question

We have proved the first statement in an earlier grade. Is the second statement true? Can you prove it?

(Hint: Draw the altitude from the vertex containing the third angle.)

Answer / Solution

Yes, the second statement is true.

The two statements referred to in the question are:

Statement 1: If two sides of a triangle are equal, then the angles opposite the equal sides are equal.

Statement 2: If two angles of a triangle are equal, then the sides opposite the equal angles have equal lengths.

We prove Statement 2.

Given: In ΔABC, ∠B = ∠C.

To prove: AC = AB.

Construction: Draw an altitude AD from A to BC. Therefore, AD ⟂ BC.

Proof: Consider ΔABD and ΔACD.

Since AD ⟂ BC, ∠ADB = ∠ADC = 90°.

Also, given ∠B = ∠C, so ∠ABD = ∠ACD.

Further, AD = AD, since AD is common to both triangles.

Therefore, ΔABD ≅ ΔACD by the AAS congruence criterion.

Hence, corresponding sides of congruent triangles are equal.

Therefore, AB = AC.

Thus, the second statement is true.

Think and Reflect

Question

It can also happen that both the proposition and converse are false! Can you give an example?

Answer / Solution

Yes.

Consider the proposition: If a number is divisible by 3, then it is divisible by 5.

This proposition is false because 3 is divisible by 3 but is not divisible by 5. Thus, 3 is a counterexample.

Its converse is: If a number is divisible by 5, then it is divisible by 3.

This is also false because 5 is divisible by 5 but is not divisible by 3. Thus, 5 is a counterexample.

Therefore, both the proposition and its converse are false.

Think and Reflect

Question 1

Identify real-life examples in which a proposition is true but not the converse. Give nice counterexamples!

Answer / Solution

Proposition: If a person is a mother, then the person is a woman.

This proposition is true.

Converse: If a person is a woman, then the person is a mother.

This converse is false.

Counterexample: A woman who does not have children.

Therefore, this is an example of a proposition that is true but whose converse is false.

Think and Reflect

Question 2

Give more examples from geometry as well as number theory in which a proposition is true but not its converse. Give appropriate counterexamples.

Answer / Solution

Geometry

Proposition: If a quadrilateral is a square, then all its sides are equal.

This is true because all four sides of a square are equal.

Converse: If all the sides of a quadrilateral are equal, then it is a square.

This is false.

Counterexample: A rhombus has all four sides equal, but it need not be a square.

Number Theory

Proposition: If a number is a multiple of 6, then it is a multiple of 3.

This is true because 6 = 2 × 3, so every multiple of 6 is also a multiple of 3.

Converse: If a number is a multiple of 3, then it is a multiple of 6.

This is false.

Counterexample: 3 is a multiple of 3 but is not a multiple of 6.

Exercise Set 9.1

Exercise Set 9.1

Frame the converse for each of the propositions in Questions 1–12. Then, determine if each of the two statements is true or not. Justify the true statements and give a counterexample for each false statement.

Question 1

If two lines are parallel, then the corresponding angles formed by a transversal are equal.

Solution

Proposition: True. When a transversal intersects two parallel lines, the corresponding angles are equal.

Converse: If the corresponding angles formed by a transversal are equal, then the two lines are parallel.

Converse: True. Equal corresponding angles formed by a transversal are a condition for the two lines to be parallel.

Therefore, both the proposition and its converse are true.

Question 2

If a quadrilateral is a square, then all its angles are equal.

Solution

Proposition: True. Every angle of a square is 90°. Therefore, all four angles are equal.

Converse: If all the angles of a quadrilateral are equal, then the quadrilateral is a square.

Converse: False.

Counterexample: A rectangle which is not a square has four equal angles, each equal to 90°, but its adjacent sides need not be equal. Hence, it is not a square.

Question 3

Given any ∆ABC, let us bisect the angles at B and C. The bisectors meet at the incentre I of the triangle.

Now extend the bisectors beyond I till they meet the opposite sides at E and F respectively, as shown.

Proposition: If AB = AC, then IE = IF.

Solution

Proposition: True.

Given AB = AC. Therefore, ∆ABC is isosceles and ∠ABC = ∠BCA.

Since BI and CI bisect these equal angles, ∠IBC = ∠ICB.

Therefore, in ∆BIC, IB = IC.

Now consider ∆CIE and ∆BIF.

Since BI and CI bisect equal angles, ∠ICE = ∠IBF.

Also, ∠CIE and ∠BIF are vertically opposite angles, so ∠CIE = ∠BIF.

And CI = BI.

Therefore, ∆CIE ≅ ∆BIF by the ASA congruence criterion.

Hence, by CPCT, IE = IF.

Thus, the proposition is true.

Converse: If IE = IF, then AB = AC.

Converse: False.

Counterexample: Take A = (0, 0), B = (1, 0), and C = (1, √3).

Then AB = 1, AC = 2, and BC = √3, so AB ≠ AC.

For this triangle, the angle-bisector construction gives I = ((3 − √3)/2, (√3 − 1)/2), E = ((√3 − 1)/2, (3 − √3)/2), and F = (4 − 2√3, 0).

Using the distance formula, IE² = IF² = 14 − 8√3.

Therefore, IE = IF even though AB ≠ AC. Hence, this is a counterexample to the converse.

Question 4

If x = y, then a + x = a + y, where x, y and a are any three numbers.

Solution

Proposition: True. Given x = y. Adding a to both sides gives a + x = a + y.

Converse: If a + x = a + y, then x = y.

Subtracting a from both sides gives a + x − a = a + y − a, and hence x = y.

Therefore, both the proposition and its converse are true.

Question 5

If a and b are perfect squares, then ab is a perfect square.

Solution

Proposition: True. Since a and b are perfect squares, there exist integers m and n such that a = m² and b = n².

Therefore, ab = m²n² = (mn)². Thus, ab is a perfect square.

Converse: If ab is a perfect square, then a and b are perfect squares.

Converse: False.

Counterexample: Take a = 2 and b = 8. Then ab = 16 = 4², which is a perfect square. However, neither 2 nor 8 is a perfect square.

Question 6

If x = y, then x² = y².

Solution

Proposition: True. Given x = y. Squaring both sides gives x² = y².

Converse: If x² = y², then x = y.

Converse: False.

Counterexample: Take x = 1 and y = −1. Then x² = 1 and y² = 1, so x² = y², but x ≠ y.

Question 7

If x = y, then x³ = y³.

Solution

Proposition: True. Given x = y. Cubing both sides gives x³ = y³.

Converse: If x³ = y³, then x = y.

Converse: True. Taking the unique real cube root of both sides gives x = y.

Question 8

If n is divisible by 24, then it is divisible by both 4 and 6.

Solution

Proposition: True. If n = 24k, then n = 4(6k) and n = 6(4k). Therefore, n is divisible by both 4 and 6.

Converse: If n is divisible by both 4 and 6, then it is divisible by 24.

Converse: False. Counterexample: n = 12. It is divisible by both 4 and 6, but not by 24.

Question 9

If n is divisible by 60, then it is divisible by both 5 and 12.

Solution

Proposition: True. If n = 60k, then n = 5(12k) and n = 12(5k). Therefore, n is divisible by both 5 and 12.

Converse: If n is divisible by both 5 and 12, then it is divisible by 60.

Converse: True. Since 5 and 12 are coprime, LCM(5, 12) = 60. Therefore, a number divisible by both 5 and 12 is divisible by 60.

Question 10

If n is the square of a prime number, then it has exactly 3 factors.

Solution

Proposition: True. Let p be prime. Then n = p², whose factors are 1, p and p². Hence, it has exactly 3 factors.

Converse: If n has exactly 3 factors, then it is the square of a prime number.

Converse: True. An integer with an odd number of factors is a perfect square. Thus n = p². Since its only factors are 1, p and p², p must be prime.

Question 11

If n is a product of two unequal prime numbers, then it has exactly 4 divisors.

Solution

Proposition: True. Let n = pq, where p and q are unequal primes. Its four positive divisors are 1, p, q and pq.

Converse: If n has exactly 4 divisors, then n is a product of two unequal prime numbers.

Converse: False. Counterexample: n = 8 has divisors 1, 2, 4 and 8, but 8 = 2³ is not a product of two unequal primes.

Question 12

If n and n + 3 have no factors in common, then n is not a multiple of 3.

Solution

Proposition: True. If n = 3k, then n + 3 = 3(k + 1), so 3 is a common factor. Therefore, if they have no common factor greater than 1, n cannot be a multiple of 3.

Converse: If n is not a multiple of 3, then n and n + 3 have no factors in common.

Converse: True. Any common divisor of n and n + 3 divides their difference, 3. Hence it can only be 1 or 3. Since 3 does not divide n, the only common factor is 1.

Question 13

There are no known ‘neat’ expressions that generate only primes! Find counterexamples to the following claims.

(i) All numbers of the form 4n² + 1 are prime.

(ii) All numbers of the form n² + n + 11 are prime.

(iii) All numbers of the form 4ⁿ + 3 are prime.

Solution

(i) Take n = 4.

4n² + 1 = 4(4²) + 1 = 65 = 5 × 13.

Therefore, 65 is composite. Hence, n = 4 is a counterexample.

(ii) Take n = 10.

n² + n + 11 = 10² + 10 + 11 = 121 = 11².

Therefore, 121 is composite. Hence, n = 10 is a counterexample.

(iii) Take n = 4.

4ⁿ + 3 = 4⁴ + 3 = 259 = 7 × 37.

Therefore, 259 is composite. Hence, n = 4 is a counterexample.

Question 14

Find counterexamples to the following statements.

(i) If n is a prime number, then 2ⁿ − 1 is a prime number.

(ii) If n is an even number, then 2ⁿ + 1 is a prime number.

Solution

(i) Take the prime number n = 11.

2¹¹ − 1 = 2047 = 23 × 89.

Therefore, 2047 is composite. Hence, n = 11 is a counterexample.

(ii) Take the even number n = 6.

2⁶ + 1 = 65 = 5 × 13.

Therefore, 65 is composite. Hence, n = 6 is a counterexample.

Question 15

Consider the statement: ‘If a number is divisible by 8, then it is divisible by both 2 and 4’.

(i) Justify the statement.

(ii) Recall the divisibility shortcuts that we have studied for different numbers. To check whether a given number is divisible by 8, is it enough to check whether it is divisible by 2 and 4? Why or why not?

Solution

(i) Suppose n is divisible by 8. Then n = 8k for some integer k.

Since 8 = 2 × 4, n = 2(4k) = 4(2k). Therefore, n is divisible by both 2 and 4.

(ii) No, it is not enough.

Consider n = 12. It is divisible by 2 and 4, but it is not divisible by 8. Therefore, checking divisibility by 2 and 4 is not sufficient to check divisibility by 8.

Question 16

Recall that a shortcut to check whether a given number is divisible by 3 is to add the digits of the number and check if the sum is a multiple of 3. Express the relationship between ‘a number is divisible by 3’ and ‘sum of the digits is a multiple of 3’ using ‘If-then’ sentences.

Solution

If a number is divisible by 3, then the sum of its digits is a multiple of 3.

Converse: If the sum of the digits of a number is a multiple of 3, then the number is divisible by 3.

Both statements are true.

Question 17

We have identified different types of quadrilaterals — squares, rectangles, parallelograms, rhombi, kites and trapezia. One can identify more types (e.g., we could create a category of quadrilaterals that have equal-length opposite sides).

Suppose we have identified a category of quadrilaterals called Q, and we have to construct a quadrilateral of this type. For this, we are to use two thin sticks, put them together as diagonals so that the quadrilateral obtained by joining their endpoints is of type Q (see the Fig. 9.2).

(i) Suppose Q satisfies the following property.

If a quadrilateral is of type Q, then it has equal-length diagonals.

(a) Should the two sticks be of equal length? Why or why not?

(b) Will it matter how the two sticks are put together?

(ii) Instead of the property mentioned above, suppose Q satisfies the following property.

If a quadrilateral has equal diagonals, then it is of type Q.

What will be your answers to (a) and (b) now?

Solution

(i)(a) Yes. The two sticks represent the diagonals. Since every quadrilateral of type Q has equal-length diagonals, equal-length sticks are necessary if we want to construct a quadrilateral satisfying the required property.

(i)(b) Yes, it may matter. The statement only gives Q ⇒ equal diagonals. It does not say equal diagonals ⇒ Q. Therefore, equal-length sticks alone may not guarantee that the resulting quadrilateral is of type Q. Their placement can matter depending on the definition of Q.

(ii)(a) Equal-length sticks are sufficient, because then the constructed quadrilateral has equal diagonals and the given property guarantees that it is of type Q. However, they are not necessarily required if Q may also contain quadrilaterals whose diagonals are unequal.

(ii)(b) For two equal-length sticks, the exact manner in which they are crossed does not affect the stated conclusion, provided their endpoints form a quadrilateral. The diagonals remain equal, so the given property guarantees that the quadrilateral is of type Q.
