Exploration: Entering the World of Secondary Science

This opening chapter introduces the way secondary science is approached: observations become measurements, patterns can be represented with symbols and equations, models simplify complex systems, and scientific ideas are tested against evidence.

Updated 28 September 2026 · By Arjun Bairva

CHAPTER OVERVIEW

What this chapter is about

The chapter focuses less on memorising scientific facts and more on understanding how scientific knowledge is developed, represented, tested and refined.

Scientific inquiry

Science begins with careful observation and questions about the natural world. Evidence, reasoning and testing help distinguish a scientific explanation from a guess.

Models and simplification

Real systems can be extremely complex. A scientific model deliberately simplifies a system so that the features relevant to a particular question can be studied.

Scientific language

Science uses agreed terms, symbols, units and mathematical relationships to communicate observations and ideas precisely.

Predictions

A scientific idea can be used to make predictions. Comparing those predictions with observations provides evidence about whether the idea and its assumptions are useful.

Estimation

Estimation helps when exact values are difficult, unnecessary or unavailable. A sensible estimate should use reasonable assumptions and an appropriate scale.

Connected branches

Physics, chemistry, biology, earth science and other areas overlap because natural systems do not follow the boundaries created for organising scientific study.

CORE IDEAS

Remember these distinctions

Observation vs inference

Observation records what is directly noticed or measured. An inference is a conclusion drawn from observations and available evidence.

Model vs reality

A model is not a complete copy of reality. It keeps selected features and may ignore others so that a particular problem can be understood.

Prediction vs guess

A prediction is linked to an idea, model or evidence and can be checked against observations. A guess does not necessarily have that testable basis.

Evidence and revision

When observations do not agree with a prediction, scientists examine measurements, assumptions and models and may revise an explanation.

QUICK REVISION

Five questions to ask yourself

1. What was observed?

Separate the actual observation or measurement from the explanation attached to it.

2. What is being measured?

Identify the physical quantity, the unit and the measurement needed to answer the question.

3. What model is being used?

Ask what the model keeps, what it simplifies and why that simplification is useful.

4. What prediction follows?

Use the stated idea or model to determine what should happen under the given conditions.

5. Does evidence support it?

Compare the prediction with the observation and identify what should be revised if they disagree.
